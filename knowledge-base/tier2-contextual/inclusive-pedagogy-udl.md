# Inclusive Pedagogy and Universal Design for Learning (UDL)

## TILT Framework — Transparent Assignment Design (Winkelmes et al., 2016)

RCT with 1,800 students and 35 faculty at 7+ institutions. Two transparently redesigned take-home assignments produced statistically significant gains (p < .05):
- Academic confidence: ES = 0.30 for first-generation students
- Sense of belonging: ES = 0.32 for first-generation students
- Mastery of employer-valued skills: medium-to-large effect sizes for first-generation, multiracial, and low-income students
- Retention: 7.5% withdrawal rate in transparent courses vs. 9.2% in control courses
- At UNLV: 90.2% year-2 retention in transparent intro courses vs. 74.1% institutional average

What makes assignments "transparent": (1) Purpose — why this assignment, explicitly linked to course goals; (2) Task — clear step-by-step instructions; (3) Criteria — explicit rubric or annotated examples of successful work.

What underserved students most valued: knowing the purpose of each assignment, knowing evaluation criteria in advance, seeing annotated examples of past student work, having tools to self-assess quality.

Faculty benefits reported: higher-level class discussions, more on-time submissions, fewer grade disputes, increased student motivation.

Implementation: use the TILT assignment template (tiltHigherEd.org) — revise just two assignments per term to start. This is a high-impact, low-effort intervention.

## Universal Design for Learning — UDL 3.0 (CAST, 2024)

UDL is a framework for designing flexible learning environments that support all learners by reducing barriers before they arise, rather than accommodating them after the fact. Goal: learner agency — purposeful, reflective, resourceful, authentic, strategic, action-oriented.

Three design principles:
1. MULTIPLE MEANS OF ENGAGEMENT — Welcoming Interests & Identities (7): optimize choice/autonomy, relevance/authenticity, joy/play; address bias, threats, distractions. Sustaining Effort & Persistence (8): clarify goals, optimize challenge, foster belonging and collaboration, offer action-oriented feedback. Emotional Capacity (9): recognize expectations/motivations, develop self-awareness, cultivate empathy.

2. MULTIPLE MEANS OF REPRESENTATION — Perception (1): support customizable display; multiple ways to perceive info; diverse perspectives. Language & Symbols (2): clarify vocabulary; support decoding; cultivate cross-language respect; address bias in symbols; use multiple media. Building Knowledge (3): connect prior knowledge; highlight patterns/big ideas; cultivate multiple ways of knowing; maximize transfer.

3. MULTIPLE MEANS OF ACTION AND EXPRESSION — Interaction (4): vary response/navigation methods; optimize access to assistive technologies. Expression & Communication (5): use multiple media and tools; build fluency with graduated support. Strategy Development (6): set meaningful goals; plan for challenges; organize information; monitor progress; challenge exclusionary practices.

Practical UDL moves for faculty: offer multiple ways to demonstrate learning (not just exams), provide content in more than one format, give students choice in discussion formats, make rubrics available before assignments, caption all videos, design for flexible pacing where possible.

## Inclusive Teaching Practices (Hogan & Sathy, 2022)

- Non-content instructor talk (NCIT) is a powerful equity lever: statements that build the instructor-student relationship, establish classroom culture, explain pedagogical choices, share personal experiences, and unmask disciplinary norms all increase belonging and performance — especially for underrepresented students.
- Types of NCIT: demonstrating respect, revealing "secrets to success," boosting self-efficacy, pre-framing activities, sharing personal stakes in the discipline, indicating it's okay to be wrong.
- Structure is the most consistent predictor of inclusive outcomes: structured activities (think-pair-share, assigned groups, explicit discussion protocols) reduce inequities in participation vs. open/voluntary formats.
- Micro-feedback strategies: end-of-class polls, thumbs up/down, open anonymous survey links posted on LMS reduce the end-of-term feedback gap and allow mid-course corrections.
- Formal midsemester evaluations: more actionable than end-of-course evaluations; administer during class time; increase completion rates by linking to student reflection on learning goals.
- Inclusive reflection questions: "In what ways has your instructor demonstrated they care about your learning?" "Is there content that has made you feel included or excluded?" "How might the class climate be more inclusive?"
- Peer observation for inclusive practice: use objective coding tools (COPUS — Classroom Observation Protocol for Undergraduate STEM) rather than impressionistic feedback. Observe across a full class session; review the syllabus and LMS too.
- Inclusive syllabus signals: statement of high expectations + confidence students can meet them; evidence of formative practice opportunities; explicit welcome to students from diverse backgrounds; accessibility commitment.

## DEI Course Design Considerations

- Audit course materials for representation: whose voices and perspectives are centered? Whose are absent?
- Design assessments that allow students to connect course content to their own lived experiences, where appropriate.
- Revisit grading weights: high-stakes single assessments disadvantage students with external demands; distributed, scaffolded assessments improve equity of outcomes.
- Explicitly name inclusive norms in the syllabus and on the first day (not just in a "land acknowledgment" or boilerplate statement) — model what intellectual community looks like.
