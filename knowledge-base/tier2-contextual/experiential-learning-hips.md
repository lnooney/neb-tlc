# Experiential Learning and High-Impact Practices (HIPs)

## Experiential Learning Theory — Kolb (Kolb & Kolb, 2017)

ELT synthesizes Dewey, Piaget, Vygotsky, Lewin, and others. Core claim: learning is the process of creating knowledge through the transformation of experience. The four-mode learning cycle:
1. Concrete Experience (CE) — direct, personal, felt experience
2. Reflective Observation (RO) — observing and reflecting on that experience from multiple perspectives
3. Abstract Conceptualization (AC) — drawing generalizations, forming theories
4. Active Experimentation (AE) — testing those theories in new situations

Full-cycle learning means moving through all four modes, not stopping at experience or reflection alone. A common failure mode in higher education: lecture-based courses engage AC but bypass CE and RO, leaving students unable to apply or transfer knowledge.

Teaching around the learning cycle means: providing direct experience (cases, simulations, field work, labs), structured reflection (journaling, discussion, analysis), conceptual framing (theory, models), and application opportunities (projects, problem sets, real-world contexts).

Experiential learning is NOT just outdoor education or role-plays — even a well-designed lecture can be experiential when paired with reflection and application. The integration of classroom and co-curricular experiences (internships, service learning, research) is critical for career readiness. Selingo (2016): 79% of the most career-successful college graduates had at least one college internship plus other experiential learning.

## High-Impact Practices — AAC&U (Association of American Colleges & Universities)

HIPs are teaching and learning practices with documented evidence of significant educational benefits — especially for historically underserved students. They work because they require significant investment of time and effort, interaction with faculty and peers, engagement with diversity, frequent feedback, and real-world application.

The 11 recognized HIPs:
1. First-Year Seminars and Experiences — small group, critical inquiry, frequent writing, faculty contact
2. Common Intellectual Experiences — shared curriculum, integrative themes across disciplines
3. Learning Communities — students take 2+ linked courses together; explore common questions across disciplines
4. Writing-Intensive Courses — writing at all levels, for different audiences, with revision; parallel efforts in quantitative reasoning, oral communication
5. Collaborative Assignments and Projects — learning to solve problems with others; sharpening understanding through diverse perspectives
6. Undergraduate Research — engaging students with contested questions, empirical observation, cutting-edge methods; strong NSF/disciplinary support in STEM
7. Diversity/Global Learning — exploring cultures, life experiences, worldviews different from one's own; often includes intercultural study abroad or community engagement
8. ePortfolios — collecting work over time, reflecting on growth, sharing with faculty, advisors, employers; especially powerful when linked to other HIPs
9. Service Learning / Community-Based Learning — applying course content in community settings with structured reflection; builds civic capacity
10. Internships — direct experience in work settings with faculty-supervised projects
11. Capstone Courses and Projects — culminating integrative experiences; research papers, portfolios, performances, exhibits

HIPs work best when they are: (a) offered to all students, not just high-achievers; (b) embedded in required curricula, not only elective; (c) designed with explicit reflection components; (d) connected to other course elements rather than isolated. First-generation and underrepresented students benefit most — and are currently least likely to participate.

## Restructuring Large Classes for Community (MacGregor, 2022)

Large lecture classes can be restructured to create learning community dynamics:
- Break courses into smaller, stable learning teams (5–7 students) that persist across weeks — accountability and belonging increase.
- Design team-based assignments around real questions or problems in the discipline.
- Use collaborative note-taking, peer instruction (think-pair-share, problem-solving), and team presentations.
- Faculty role shifts from sole knowledge source to facilitator and expert guide.
- Peer learning in well-structured groups produces gains comparable to smaller classes — the key is structure, not group size.
