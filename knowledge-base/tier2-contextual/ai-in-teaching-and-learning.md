# AI in Teaching and Learning

NOTE: The AI in education field is evolving rapidly. These findings reflect research through mid-2025. Recommend faculty also consult current institutional guidance from BU IETL (excellence@bu.edu) and BU's Academic Technology office for the most current policy landscape.

## Student AI Use in Assessments (Ott et al., 2024)

Case study: 25 undergraduates using ChatGPT 4.0 during 4 formative e-assessments in a communication research methods course. Key findings:
- 40–47% of students used ChatGPT for at least some assessment questions, depending on assessment.
- Most common motivations: knowledge gaps (didn't know the answer), need for clarification/definitions, verification of existing answers, and convenience of quick responses.
- Adoption rate after using ChatGPT was very high: 85–98% of students who used it accepted the AI-generated answer.
- Error rate: a small but non-trivial percentage of adopted ChatGPT answers were incorrect (3–7% per assessment), rising to 6.7% in Assessment 4.
- Students most commonly pasted the entire assessment question directly into ChatGPT (copy-paste prompting).
- Traditional resources (notes, textbook) still preferred overall, but gap narrowed over the semester as reliance on ChatGPT increased.

Assessment design implications: Question types that require synthesis, application, or original analysis are less susceptible to AI-assisted cheating than recall-based multiple choice and true/false. Explicit AI policy in the syllabus is necessary but not sufficient — students need AI literacy training and clear guidance on when and how AI use is appropriate.

## Faculty Perspectives on Generative AI (Acuyo Cespedes, 2025)

Qualitative study: 41 faculty at an English-medium university. Key themes:

Benefits perceived by faculty:
- AI is a paradigm shift that is here to stay — proactive adaptation is preferable to resistance.
- When used as a complement (not replacement), AI can boost faculty productivity in repetitive tasks: quiz design, lesson planning, generating first drafts of rubrics.
- Students benefit from AI as a brainstorming scaffold — helps them overcome the "blank page" problem and unblock creative impasses in writing.
- The human dimensions of teaching (empathy, adaptability, inspiration) are not replaceable by AI.

Challenges perceived by faculty:
- Institutional policy lag: most universities lack coherent AI guidance for assessment, academic integrity, and classroom use.
- Assessment rubrics and grading criteria need revision to reflect AI-assisted learning contexts — e.g., rubrics that assess grammatical accuracy may be moot when AI can correct grammar instantly.
- "Bypassing the learning struggle": when students use AI to skip productive struggle, skill development suffers — especially higher-order thinking, argumentation, and creativity.
- AI output limitations: hallucinations, bias, "polished but wrong" outputs increase risk when students lack the domain knowledge to evaluate AI claims.
- Policing burden: detecting AI-assisted plagiarism increases faculty workload disproportionately.

## Actionable Recommendations for Faculty Designing in an AI Context

1. Design assessments that require process artifacts (drafts, reflections, in-class components) that AI cannot easily replicate.
2. Shift toward oral defenses, presentations, and in-person components for high-stakes assessment.
3. Teach AI literacy explicitly — help students understand how to evaluate, prompt, and critically assess AI outputs.
4. Write a clear, specific AI policy for your syllabus: define what uses are permitted, how to disclose AI use (APA Style for AI citation is now established), and what constitutes misuse.
5. Use AI-resistant question types: open-ended reflection connected to class-specific discussions, problems requiring course-specific data, peer review with accountability.
6. Collaborate with colleagues: course-level and program-level coordination on AI policy prevents students from navigating inconsistent expectations.
