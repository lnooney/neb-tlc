# Course and Syllabus Design

## Learner-Centered Syllabus (UC San Diego Learner-Centered Syllabus Guide)

A learner-centered syllabus sets the tone for a course and communicates expectations as a partnership, not a list of rules. It should:
- Explain not just WHAT students will learn but WHY — link the course to program goals, career relevance, and students' lived contexts.
- State learning outcomes in measurable, action-oriented language aligned with Bloom's Taxonomy.
- Describe grading and assessment criteria transparently, including rubrics or worked examples where possible.
- Communicate instructor responsiveness expectations clearly (e.g., email turnaround, feedback timelines).
- Include a reciprocal expectations table: what students can expect from the instructor, and what the instructor expects of students.
- Provide accessibility and accommodation information prominently, not buried in fine print.
- Reference support resources (tutoring, writing center, disability services, mental health) in accessible language.

## Backward Design and Motivational Syllabus (Harrington & Thomas, 2018)

Backward design (Wiggins & McTighe, 2005) is the most evidence-supported course design framework:
- Step 1: Identify desired results — What should students know, do, or value after completing the course?
- Step 2: Determine acceptable evidence — What assessments would demonstrate achievement of each outcome?
- Step 3: Plan learning experiences — What teaching methods and activities will prepare students to succeed on those assessments?

Research support: Winkelmes et al. (2016) found significant gains in student confidence and work quality when course purpose was made explicit. Rienties & Toetenel (2016) found that learning design directly affects student behavior in both blended and online courses. Wang et al. (2013) found students in outcome-aligned courses are more likely to adopt deep learning approaches.

Coverage-based design (organized around textbook chapters) and activity-based design (organized around fun activities) both fail to center learning outcomes — and often produce surface-level cognitive engagement. Backward design keeps high-level learning central.

## Syllabus as Motivation Tool

Sharing how a course fits into the overall degree or career pathway immediately adds relevance and meaning. Students in Smith & Razzouk (1993) study: nearly 30% could not recall a single course learning outcome after instruction. Making outcomes explicit, prominent, and connected to student goals increases engagement.

Key practical moves:
- Add a brief "Why this course matters" narrative to the syllabus introduction.
- Link each major assignment explicitly to at least one course learning outcome.
- Redesign the "Policies" section to frame expectations as mutual commitments, not punishments.
- Provide a course schedule that shows the conceptual arc — not just dates and topics.
