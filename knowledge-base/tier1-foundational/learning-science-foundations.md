# Learning Science Foundations

## Principle 1 — Prior Knowledge

Students' prior knowledge can help or hinder new learning. Accurate, activated prior knowledge is foundational; inert, insufficient, inappropriate, or inaccurate prior knowledge impedes learning. Misconceptions are deeply resistant to correction — they require deliberate refutation, not just additional instruction. Declarative knowledge (knowing what) vs. procedural knowledge (knowing how and when) — students often have one without the other. Even small instructional prompts significantly activate prior knowledge. Instructors should explicitly assess prior knowledge rather than assuming sufficiency.

## Principle 2 — Knowledge Organization

How students organize knowledge affects retrieval and transfer. Experts organize around deep principles; novices around surface features. Students benefit from explicit organizational structures (concept maps, matrices, hierarchies). Instructors should make their expert knowledge organization visible.

## Principle 3 — Motivation

Three interacting factors — (a) subjective value, (b) expectancy for success, (c) supportive environment. Self-efficacy is critical. Growth vs. fixed mindset affects responses to challenge and failure. Extrinsic motivators can undermine intrinsic motivation if misapplied.

## Principle 4 — Mastery Development

Developing mastery requires: (a) acquiring component skills, (b) practicing integrating those components, (c) learning when and how to apply them. Instructors often suffer from "expert blind spot." Scaffolding complex tasks into components, then gradually removing support, is essential.

## Principle 5 — Practice and Feedback

Goal-focused practice at appropriate challenge level, in sufficient quantity and frequency. Feedback must be: specific, timely, actionable, criterion-referenced. Formative feedback during learning is more valuable than summative feedback after. Rubrics and worked examples help students understand what success looks like.

## Principle 6 — Course Climate

Course climate directly and measurably affects learning. Negative climates (stereotypes, dismissiveness, identity threats) impede learning. Positive, inclusive climates energize learning. Sense of belonging and identity safety are not soft extras — they affect cognitive capacity and performance.

## Principle 7 — Self-Directed Learning / Metacognition

Students who can monitor and control their own learning learn more effectively. Students don't engage in metacognition naturally and need explicit prompting. Tools: exam wrappers, reflection prompts, concept maps, self-assessment rubrics, explicit discussion of what good learning looks like.
