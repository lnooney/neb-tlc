# Assessment and Learning Outcomes

## Key Findings — Designing and Assessing Student Learning Outcomes (SLOs)

- Assessing student learning outcomes is a shared responsibility among students, instructors, and institutions; ownership no longer rests solely with the learner.
- Challenges include definitional confusion around "assessment," difficulty standardizing measures across diverse institutional contexts, and collaboration demands across stakeholders.
- Best practice: define SLOs in measurable, action-oriented terms using Bloom's Taxonomy verbs (remember → understand → apply → analyze → evaluate → create). Every SLO should be directly assessed.
- Outcome-based instructional design — designing assessments first, then activities to support them — is more effective than coverage-based or activity-based design.
- Rubrics are especially effective for assessing authentic learning tasks and communicating expectations; consistent use across sections strengthens validity.
- Formative assessment cycles (iterative, low-stakes practice with feedback before high-stakes evaluation) consistently outperform summative-only approaches.
- Assessment culture matters: institutions and courses where faculty treat assessment as inquiry into student learning, rather than compliance, produce better outcomes.
- Micro-feedback mechanisms (brief in-class polls, open anonymous surveys, thumbs up/down) give instructors real-time data and signal to students that their input matters.
- Mid-semester evaluations are more actionable than end-of-course evaluations — using class time to administer them and responding visibly to findings increases completion rates and trust.

## Facilitation of Evidence-Based Practice (Ritchie et al., 2020)

- Expert implementation facilitators develop 22 complex, overlapping skills across five domains: (1) building relationships and supportive environments, (2) changing systems and structures, (3) transferring knowledge and creating learning infrastructure, (4) planning and leading change, (5) assessing people, processes, and outcomes.
- Facilitation skills are too complex to transfer through didactic methods alone — modeling, mentoring, and practice with feedback are essential.
- Implication for faculty: implementing new teaching strategies is an iterative process requiring support structures. Seek peer observation, teaching center consultation, and faculty learning communities rather than going it alone.
